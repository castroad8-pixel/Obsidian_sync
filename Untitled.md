### **Rationale**

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) disproportionately affects rural populations in the United States. While tobacco use remains the leading risk factor, social and structural inequities may contribute independently to geographic differences in disease burden. This study examined COPD prevalence across urban–rural settings using nationally representative data and quasibinomial regression models to determine whether observed disparities persist after accounting for demographics, healthcare access, and social determinants of health (SDOH), both overall and stratified by smoking status.

### **Methods**

Data were obtained from 40,779 U.S. adults classified as living in large metropolitan (30.9%), small/medium metropolitan (55.2%), or rural (13.8%) areas. Weighted estimates were used to characterize sociodemographic, behavioral, and healthcare access variables. COPD prevalence and relative risks (RRs) were estimated using quasibinomial models with sequential adjustments: (a) age-standardized, (b) demographic-adjusted, (c) access-adjusted, and (d) SDOH-adjusted (income, education, employment, and housing assistance). Analyses were conducted for the full cohort and separately among smokers and nonsmokers.

### **Results**

In the full sample, COPD prevalence increased from 4.9% (95% CI: 4.4–5.4%) in large metropolitan to 6.2% (5.8–6.6%) in small/medium metropolitan and 10.2% (9.2–11.4%) in rural areas. Compared with large metro adults, the rural COPD RR was 2.09 (1.89–2.59) unadjusted, 1.74 (1.48–2.04) after demographic adjustment, 1.74 (1.48–2.05) after healthcare access adjustment, and attenuated to 1.14 (0.84–1.53) after SDOH adjustment.

Among **smokers**, COPD prevalence was 9.1% (8.0–10.2%) in large metro, 11.2% (10.5–12.0%) in small/medium metro, and 17.1% (15.0–19.0%) in rural adults. Relative to large metro smokers, the rural RR was 2.05 (1.69–2.48) unadjusted, 1.76 (1.45–2.14) after demographic controls, 1.90 (1.56–2.32) after healthcare access adjustment, and 1.42 (1.16–1.73) following SDOH adjustment.

Among **nonsmokers**, COPD prevalence was lower overall—2.4% (2.0–2.8%) in large metro, 2.4% (2.1–2.7%) in small/medium metro, and 3.9% (3.1–4.6%) in rural areas. Rural–urban RRs were 1.62 (1.24–2.12) unadjusted, 1.46 (1.11–1.90) with demographic controls, 1.58 (1.20–2.08) with access adjustment, and 1.27 (0.96–1.67) in the fully SDOH-adjusted model. Dispersion values ranged from 0.998–1.011 across models, indicating good model fit.

### **Conclusions**

COPD prevalence is markedly higher among rural adults, particularly among current and former smokers. However, after accounting for socioeconomic and structural determinants, these differences largely dissipate, suggesting that geographic disparities in COPD reflect underlying inequities rather than location itself. Addressing poverty, education, and healthcare access is essential for reducing COPD burden across the urban–rural continuum.
